Stadium

One Stadium, Rebuilt From Scratch

Zaragoza brings a single stadium to 2030, and it's a genuinely new one. The historic La Romareda was demolished in 2025 after 68 years, and Real Zaragoza are playing their transition seasons at a modular temporary ground while the Nueva Romareda rises on the same site, shaped by the region's distinctive Cierzo wind.

Brand New for 2030
Nueva Romareda
Capacity ~43,000 (target) · A complete rebuild of the 1957 original on the same site, designed by IDOM around the Cierzo, the strong regional wind. Scheduled to open in 2027-2028 at a cost of around €140 million. Real Zaragoza currently plays home matches at the temporary 20,103-seat Ibercaja Stadium next door while construction finishes.

Football Culture

One City, One Club

Founded 1932 6 Copa del Rey titles 1995 Cup Winners' Cup champions 3 1982 World Cup matches hosted

⚪🔵 Real Zaragoza, Los Maños

Unlike Madrid, Barcelona or Seville, Zaragoza is a genuine one-club city - Real Zaragoza has no meaningful local rival, giving its football culture a unified, communal character rather than a divided one. Founded in 1932, the club's fans are known as maños, a nickname shared with Aragonese people generally, roughly translating to a term of familiar address among locals.

The club's greatest night came on 10 May 1995, when Zaragoza beat Arsenal 2-1 in the UEFA Cup Winners' Cup Final at Parc des Princes, settled by a spectacular 25-yard strike from Nayim in the final seconds of extra time - a goal still replayed in English football folklore as one of the most painful late strikes conceded by an Arsenal side. Zaragoza have also won the Copa del Rey six times, most recently in 2004, when they beat Real Madrid in the final.

🌬️ Built for the Cierzo

The Nueva Romareda's design takes its defining cue from the Cierzo, a strong, dry, cold wind that funnels down the Ebro valley, particularly in autumn and winter. Rather than fight it, architects IDOM shaped the stadium's roof and facade around the wind's flow, using concave and convex surfaces described as being "forged by the wind" - a rare case of a World Cup stadium designed around a specific local weather phenomenon.

The rebuild is a genuinely fresh start rather than a renovation: the old La Romareda, opened in 1957, was fully demolished after Real Zaragoza's final match there in May 2025, a 1-0 win over Deportivo de La Coruña. In the meantime, the club is playing at the Ibercaja Stadium, a modular, demountable 20,103-seat venue built in around six months - the first stadium of its kind in Spanish football history.

🏆 A World Cup Returns

The original La Romareda hosted three group-stage matches at the 1982 World Cup and staged football during the 1992 Barcelona Olympics. Its replacement will welcome the tournament back to Zaragoza for the first time in nearly half a century, in a stadium built specifically to meet FIFA's modern requirements.

A Six-Month Stadium

The Ibercaja Stadium, Real Zaragoza's temporary home, was designed and built in roughly six months - a genuinely unusual pace for a professional football venue, even a modular one.

Matchday

Where Zaragoza Watches Football

El Tubo's tapas bars show every match on big screens for fans without tickets, and the streets around the stadium fill with maño colours on matchday. As a one-club city, expect a shared, communal atmosphere rather than the divided energy of Madrid or Seville.

  • 🚊 Tram & bus - the stadium under 20 min from the centre
  • 🎉 Plaza del Pilar - Zaragoza's natural gathering point
  • 🏆 6 Copa del Rey titles & a 1995 European trophy
🌬️

Matchday in Zaragoza

"Zaragoza is a one-club city - there's no rival to argue with, just a shared devotion to Los Maños."

Zaragoza's compact centre and tram network mean the stadium area is reachable in well under 20 minutes from El Tubo or Plaza España. The build-up concentrates in the historic centre's tapas bars, where fans gather in Real Zaragoza's white and blue well before kickoff.

By 2030, the new stadium should be complete, but visitors in the transition years may still catch matches at the temporary Ibercaja Stadium next door - a genuinely unusual sight in top-level football.

Where to Stay

Three Neighbourhoods, One Compact City

Zaragoza's historic centre is small enough that almost anywhere central puts you within a tram or bus ride of the stadium. Here's how the main districts compare.

  • ⛪ El Casco Histórico - the old town, El Tubo's tapas, the Basilica
  • 🏙 Centro - modern, commercial, Paseo Independencia
  • ⚽ Estadio - the ground's own district, limited hotels
⛪
El Casco Histórico - Historic Centre
The old town around the Basilica del Pilar, home to El Tubo's dense tapas quarter and Roman-era remains. Central, atmospheric and the natural first choice.
🏙
Centro - Modern & Commercial
The city's main shopping boulevard, Paseo Independencia, and surrounding streets. Central, well-connected by tram, with a wider range of hotel options.
⚽
Estadio - Matchday Convenience
The district around the stadium itself, in the north of the city. Limited hotel stock but a genuine option for fans attending several matches.

Getting Around

A Genuine Crossroads Between Madrid and Barcelona

Zaragoza's tram line and extensive bus network cover the compact city centre easily. Its real advantage is location: the AVE high-speed line puts both Madrid and Barcelona within about 1 hour 20 minutes.

From the Airport
Zaragoza Airport (ZAZ) sits about 10km west of the centre. Bus 505 runs express to Paseo María Agustín in 30 minutes; bus 501 is a slower, cheaper alternative. Many visitors also arrive by AVE from Madrid or Barcelona instead of flying.

Between Matches

What to Do When There's No Match

Zaragoza rewards unhurried mornings around the Basilica and long tapas evenings in El Tubo. Its compact size means most sights are within easy walking distance of each other.

⛪
Basilica del Pilar
One of Spain's most important pilgrimage sites, a monumental baroque basilica on the banks of the Ebro. The riverside plaza in front is Zaragoza's social heart.
🕌
Palacio de la Aljafería
An 11th-century Moorish palace, a rare surviving example of Islamic architecture from Spain's taifa period, now also home to the regional parliament.
🍢
El Tubo
A maze of narrow streets behind the Basilica, packed with tapas bars serving Aragonese specialities. Zaragoza's best introduction to local food culture.
🚄
Madrid or Barcelona
Both just 1h20 away by AVE, making a genuine day trip to either city entirely realistic from a Zaragoza base - a rare convenience among World Cup 2030 host cities.

Food & Drink

What to Eat Before Kickoff

Aragonese cuisine centres on hearty, honest cooking - roast lamb, regional vegetables, and cheap, good wine. El Tubo's dense grid of tapas bars is the natural place to sample all of it.

  • 🐑 Ternasco de Aragón - protected-origin roast lamb
  • 🥬 Borraja - a regional leafy vegetable, often with potatoes
  • 🍢 El Tubo tapas crawl - dense, affordable, historic
  • 🍷 Local Cañamero & Somontano wines - cheap and reliable
  • 🍫 Churros con chocolate - a classic breakfast near Plaza del Pilar

Here's what to order at each budget, from a quick tapas bite to Zaragoza's best Aragonese cooking.

  • Churros con chocolate: near Plaza del Pilar, a classic breakfast
  • Caña: small draft beer, the standard tapas-bar order
  • Local wine by the glass: Somontano or Cañamero, €2-4

Near the stadium specifically, food options are limited to a handful of casual spots, so eat in El Tubo before heading out. Zaragoza's food costs are among the most affordable of any World Cup 2030 host city in Spain.

Practical Info

Before You Go

Weather, safety, language and money - the basics every fan should have sorted before landing in Zaragoza.

  • 🌡️ Hot, dry summers with the occasional Cierzo wind
  • 🛂 Schengen Area - visa-free for 60+ nationalities
  • 💶 €60-110/day comfortably covers hotel, food and transport
🌤

Weather

30-34°C in June-July, hot and dry given Zaragoza's inland Ebro valley setting. The Cierzo wind can bring occasional relief, though it's more common in autumn and winter.

🛡

Safety

Zaragoza is a safe city with low rates of violent crime. Standard pickpocket precautions apply around Plaza del Pilar and El Tubo in busy periods.

🗣

Language

Spanish is the official language; English is spoken in hotels and tourism-facing venues, though somewhat less widely than in Madrid or Barcelona.

💳

Money

Euro (€). Cards accepted almost everywhere, including tapas bars. ATMs widely available.

FAQ

Zaragoza Questions

Is the Nueva Romareda stadium finished yet?
Not as of mid-2026. The original La Romareda was demolished after its final match in May 2025, and Real Zaragoza are currently playing at a temporary 20,103-seat modular stadium, the Ibercaja Stadium, while the new ground is built on the same site. The Nueva Romareda is scheduled to open in 2027-2028 with roughly 43,000 seats.
What was wrong with the old La Romareda?
The original stadium, opened in 1957 with a capacity of 33,608, fell short of FIFA's 40,000-seat minimum for 2030 World Cup venues and needed modernising after decades of use. Rather than renovate it, Real Zaragoza chose a complete rebuild on the same site.
How do I get to the Ibercaja Stadium or Nueva Romareda?
Both stadiums sit on the same site in the north of Zaragoza, reachable by city bus or a walk from the tram network. Local bus lines connect the stadium area to Plaza España and the historic centre in around 15-20 minutes.
How many World Cup 2030 matches will Zaragoza host?
Exact fixtures depend on FIFA's official match schedule, expected around 2027-2028. As a single-stadium city with one of the smaller capacities among Spain's venues, Zaragoza is expected to host a limited number of group-stage matches.
Did Zaragoza host World Cup matches before?
Yes. The original La Romareda hosted three group-stage matches at the 1982 World Cup, and the stadium also staged football matches during the 1992 Barcelona Olympics.
What is the best neighbourhood to stay in Zaragoza for the World Cup?
El Casco Histórico (the old town), home to El Tubo's tapas bars and the Basilica del Pilar, puts you in the heart of the city and within reach of the stadium by bus or tram. Centro, around Paseo Independencia, offers a more modern, commercial base equally central.
Should I stay near the stadium or in central Zaragoza?
Stay centrally. The stadium area in the north of the city has limited hotel stock and few restaurants, while the historic centre reaches it by bus or tram in about 15-20 minutes and keeps you close to El Tubo's tapas bars and the Basilica del Pilar.
Is Zaragoza expensive compared to other World Cup 2030 host cities?
Zaragoza is one of the more affordable Spanish host cities, with budget hotel rooms outside the tournament premium typically running €60-100/night. Tapas in El Tubo remain reasonably priced even in the historic centre.
What is Real Zaragoza's football history?
Real Zaragoza has won the Copa del Rey six times and the European Cup Winners' Cup in 1995, beating Arsenal in the final on a famous long-range goal by Nayim. The club currently plays in the Segunda División, Spain's second tier, and is aiming for promotion back to La Liga around the time its new stadium opens.
Why is Real Zaragoza playing in a temporary stadium?
With La Romareda demolished for its World Cup rebuild, the club needed a home ground for the transition period. The Ibercaja Stadium, a modular 20,103-seat venue built in around six months, opened in 2025 as the first temporary demountable stadium in Spanish football history.
What is the Cierzo?
The Cierzo is a strong, cold, dry wind that blows through the Ebro valley, particularly in autumn and winter, and is a defining feature of life in Zaragoza. The Nueva Romareda's design was explicitly shaped around it, using the stadium's form to work with the wind rather than against it.
Is Zaragoza a big football city?
Zaragoza is a one-club city in football terms, unlike Madrid, Barcelona or Seville. Real Zaragoza is the sole major club, giving the city a more unified, less rivalry-driven football culture than many other Spanish host cities.
What is the weather like in Zaragoza in June and July?
Hot and dry, typically 30-34°C in the afternoons, with the Cierzo wind offering occasional relief. Zaragoza's inland Ebro valley location means summers are intense, though slightly less extreme than Seville or Madrid.
What should I eat in Zaragoza?
Try ternasco de Aragón, a protected-origin roast lamb dish, and borraja, a regional leafy vegetable often served with potatoes. El Tubo, the tapas quarter behind the Basilica del Pilar, is the natural place to sample small plates and local wine.
Is Zaragoza safe for tourists during the World Cup?
Yes, Zaragoza is considered a safe city with low rates of violent crime, and standard pickpocket precautions are sufficient in the busiest parts of the historic centre and around Plaza del Pilar.
Can I day-trip from Zaragoza during the World Cup?
Zaragoza's biggest advantage is its location almost exactly halfway between Madrid and Barcelona on the AVE high-speed line, each reachable in about 1 hour 20 minutes. This makes Zaragoza a genuinely practical base for fans splitting matches between multiple host cities.
