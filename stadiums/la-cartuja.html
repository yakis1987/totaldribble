Architecture & Design

A Stadium Built for a Games That Never Came

La Cartuja, officially Estadio de La Cartuja de Sevilla and originally Estadio Olímpico de Sevilla, was designed by Cruz y Ortiz Arquitectos, the Seville firm also behind Madrid's Cívitas Metropolitano, and completed in 1999 for Seville's bids to host the 2004 and 2008 Summer Olympics. Both bids failed, leaving the city with a modern, publicly funded athletics stadium and no permanent tenant: neither Real Betis nor Sevilla FC ever moved in, both preferring their own historic grounds.

The original design gave the stadium a steel and polycarbonate roof stretched over the seating decks, sheltering fans from Andalusia's punishing summer heat. At opening, its capacity of around 60,000 made it one of Spain's largest stadiums, though the running track that made it viable for athletics also kept fans frustratingly distant from the pitch for football, a compromise shared by other Olympic-legacy venues built for the same era, including Grand Stade de Marrakech in Morocco.

For two decades, the stadium found purpose mainly as an occasional-use venue: hosting the 1999 World Athletics Championships, the 2003 UEFA Cup final, national team fixtures, Copa del Rey finals, and major concerts, while sitting largely dormant the rest of the year. That changed once Spain's 2030 World Cup bid gained momentum and FIFA's football-specific requirements gave the stadium's long-delayed transformation real urgency.

The complex was designed for more than football from the start. Beyond matchdays, La Cartuja functions as a genuine multi-use venue: ramps, lifts, accessible restrooms and dedicated wheelchair seating areas run throughout the bowl, and the surrounding halls have hosted everything from the DIVERSA Pride Festival to business conferences and product launches, alongside an on-site medical clinic and corporate event spaces built into the original 1999 structure.

The stadium's UEFA Elite Stadium certification, the same tier required to host a Champions League or Europa League final, rests partly on its broadcast infrastructure: floodlights exceeding 2,500 lux, more than 18 camera positions around the pitch, two 84m² high-resolution video scoreboards and 48 private boxes. The pitch itself is natural Ryegrass rather than a hybrid surface, a deliberate choice given how few days a year the turf actually sees competitive football, replanted each September after a summer of concerts.

The stadium's island setting is itself a legacy of Expo '92, the 1992 Seville World's Fair that reshaped Isla de la Cartuja from open land into a purpose-built district, complete with new bridges, gardens and the research park now known as Cartuja 93. That neutral ground has served the city well since: as a venue with no historical tie to either Real Betis or Sevilla FC, La Cartuja is the natural host whenever Seville needs a stadium above the fray of its oldest football rivalry, from cup finals to national team fixtures.

Renovation & Fan Experience

Two Decades of Waiting, Nine Months of Building

Phase 1 removed the athletics track in under a year. Phase 2 will finish the job by 2028, with a redesigned roof and facade to match.

Betis Match Tickets Explore the Architecture

Once Spain's 2030 World Cup bid gained momentum, La Cartuja's fortunes changed fast. Phase 1 construction began in July 2024 and finished just before the 26 April 2025 Copa del Rey final, removing the long-criticised athletics track, lowering the pitch, and adding a new Grada Baja lower tier, raising capacity from 57,600 to roughly 70,000 for a strikingly modest cost of around €15 million.

The rushed Phase 1 timeline came with growing pains: some fans complained about disorganisation at the 2025 Copa del Rey final, and FIFA reportedly flagged concerns. Local authorities describe it as a one-off issue tied to the compressed schedule, and Phase 2, targeting 2028 completion and a final capacity Cruz y Ortiz cites at 71,374, is intended to iron out those problems permanently, with most of the heaviest work scheduled once Real Betis and Sevilla FC return to their own rebuilt stadiums.

What We Don't Know Yet

The exact final capacity after Phase 2 has been reported anywhere from 70,000 to 75,000, and Cruz y Ortiz's own materials cite 71,374. We'll update this page once a confirmed final figure is published.

Matchday Atmosphere

Betis Green and White on an Olympic Stage

A club with a century of passion, adapting to a bigger, newer home. The Gol Sur end has become Béticos' new heartbeat, drums and flags carried over from Villamarín.

See Tickets Full Seville Guide

With Real Betis playing every home match at La Cartuja through at least the 2027-28 season while their own Benito Villamarín is rebuilt, the club's famously loyal Béticos have had to relearn matchday habits in a stadium roughly a third larger than their old ground. The Gol Sur end has become the adopted home of the club's most vocal supporters, filled with flags, drums and constant singing behind the goal, while the Preferencia and Fondo sides along the touchline offer a calmer, more panoramic view of the pitch.

The scale of the place still shows its Olympic origins: rows sit further back than at a purpose-built football ground, and the Segundo Anfiteatro upper tier, part of the original 1999 structure, puts fans noticeably higher than the new lower-tier seating built directly over the old running track. Betis has responded by doubling its VIP hospitality footprint for the La Cartuja seasons, a practical nod to the stadium's larger capacity and UEFA Elite-category standing.

The Copa del Rey final has become an annual fixture here too: Real Sociedad beat Atlético Madrid 4-3 on penalties after a 2-2 draw in the 2026 final, continuing a run of finals hosted at the venue every year since 2020, several of them Clásico or Andalusian derby occasions that filled all four stands.

History & Significance

From Empty Olympic Dream to Cup Final Fixture

Nearly dormant for twenty years, now one of Spanish football's busiest neutral grounds. A UEFA Cup final, seven Copa del Rey finals and counting.

See All Stadiums See Spain Guide

La Cartuja's Athletics World Championships debut in 1999 gave way to genuine football pedigree over the following decades, most notably the 2003 UEFA Cup final, when tens of thousands of Celtic supporters descended on Seville only to watch their side lose to FC Porto. The stadium replaced San Mamés as a Euro 2020 host venue after Bilbao couldn't guarantee attendance during the pandemic, and has hosted the Copa del Rey final continuously since 2020, with the RFEF confirming in November 2025 that the venue will keep the fixture for three more editions.

La Cartuja is one of Spain's confirmed 2030 World Cup venues, alongside Santiago Bernabéu in Madrid and Estádio da Luz across the border in Lisbon, part of the shared Spain-Portugal-Morocco hosting bloc.

The stadium sits in Seville's Isla de la Cartuja district. Its list of concert performers reads like a greatest-hits of stadium touring acts: Madonna, U2, Bruce Springsteen, AC/DC, and the Red Hot Chili Peppers, who opened their entire 2022 Global Stadium Tour there. In June 2022, Spanish singer Manuel Carrasco set the record for Spain's most-attended single-day concert at the venue, with 74,345 tickets sold.

World Cup 2030 Role

Andalusia's Flagship Venue

The only World Cup 2030 venue south of Madrid, Extremadura or Murcia. Andalusia's 8.4 million people funnel through one stadium in 2030.

Flight Deals Full Seville Guide

As the sole 2030 World Cup venue in Andalusia, and one of the entire southern half of the Iberian Peninsula, La Cartuja gives the region a genuine flagship, backed by a proven track record hosting UEFA Elite-category fixtures and major national finals. FIFA's minimum requirements call for 40,000 net seats for the group stage and at least 60,000 for a semifinal, figures La Cartuja clears comfortably at its post-renovation capacity.

Seville's mayor has spoken confidently about the city's ambitions for the tournament's later rounds, though officials have been careful to clarify exactly what role is realistic. The stadium's temporary role as home to Real Betis, and potentially Sevilla FC during its own stadium redevelopment, only deepens its integration into the city's football life ahead of 2030, while ongoing work to improve rail and road access to the Isla de la Cartuja remains the main outstanding item on FIFA's checklist.

Getting There

On the Isla de la Cartuja

An island venue with strictly controlled matchday road access. Metro, bus and a short taxi ride are the practical options once the gates open.

Search Seville Hotels Compare Flights

La Cartuja sits on Seville's Isla de la Cartuja, a district on the Guadalquivir River just north of the historic centre, roughly 2-4km from the city's core depending on route, reachable by metro, bus and taxi, with an on-site four-star hotel offering pitch-facing rooms for fans wanting to stay right at the venue.

Metro
Line 1 to Blas Infante station, then a matchday shuttle bus every 5 minutes to the stadium.
Bus
The C1/C2 circular lines connect Santa Justa station and the city centre directly to the ground.
Taxi
Roughly 15-20 minutes from Santa Cruz or Centro, longer after the final whistle as the island empties out.
Walk
30-40 minutes from central districts like Alameda, a pleasant riverside option before kickoff.

Frequently Asked

La Cartuja Questions

Who designed La Cartuja?
Cruz y Ortiz Arquitectos, the same firm behind Cívitas Metropolitano in Madrid, designed the stadium, which was completed in 1999 for Seville's Olympic bids.
Why did La Cartuja never have a permanent club?
Both Real Betis and Sevilla FC were invited to move in after the failed Olympic bids, but both preferred their own historic stadiums, leaving La Cartuja without a resident tenant for most of its history.
What was the stadium originally built for?
Seville's bids to host the 2004 and 2008 Summer Olympics, both unsuccessful, which is why the stadium was originally built with a full athletics track around the pitch.
What amenities does the stadium complex include?
A four-star hotel, EXE Isla Cartuja, with 72 rooms, some overlooking the pitch and others with city views, located within the stadium complex itself.
What is the capacity of La Cartuja?
70,000 seats following the 2025 Phase 1 renovation, up from 57,600, making it the fourth-largest stadium in Spain and the largest in Andalusia.
How did the renovation happen so quickly and cheaply?
Phase 1 focused specifically on removing the athletics track, lowering the pitch and adding a new lower-tier stand, work completed for around €15 million between July 2024 and April 2025, a notably efficient scope compared to full stadium rebuilds elsewhere in Spain.
What does Phase 2 of the renovation involve?
A larger, roughly €100 million project covering an enlarged roof, renovated facade, upgraded interior spaces and new seating throughout, targeted for completion by 2028.
Were there problems with the 2025 renovation?
Some fans complained about disorganisation at the April 2025 Copa del Rey final, and FIFA reportedly flagged concerns, though authorities describe it as a one-off issue tied to the compressed construction schedule ahead of that match.
When did La Cartuja open?
1999, hosting the World Athletics Championships shortly after completion.
Has La Cartuja hosted major European finals?
Yes, the 2003 UEFA Cup final, where FC Porto beat Celtic in front of nearly 53,000 fans, with roughly 70,000 Celtic supporters travelling to Seville that year even though not all could fit in the stadium.
Which club plays at La Cartuja now?
Real Betis, as a temporary tenant from the 2025-26 season while its own stadium, Benito Villamarín, undergoes redevelopment, with a return targeted for the 2028-29 season.
What was the result of the 2026 Copa del Rey final?
Real Sociedad beat Atlético Madrid 4-3 on penalties after a 2-2 draw following extra time, on 18 April 2026, winning the club's fourth Copa del Rey title.
How do I get to La Cartuja from central Seville?
By Metro Line 1 to Blas Infante station plus a shuttle bus, by the C1/C2 circular bus lines, or by taxi, roughly 15-20 minutes from the historic centre.
What's the nearest airport to La Cartuja?
Seville Airport (SVQ), roughly 25-30 minutes from the stadium by taxi.
Can I stay right at the stadium?
Yes, the on-site EXE Isla Cartuja Hotel offers 72 rooms, some with pitch views, directly within the stadium complex.
How do I buy tickets for a Real Betis match at La Cartuja?
Through the club's official site, where Soy Bético members get priority access before any remaining tickets go to general sale a few days before kickoff.
