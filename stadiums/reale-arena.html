Architecture & Design

From Athletics Bowl to Intimate Arena

The stadium originally known as Anoeta opened on 13 August 1993, designed by architects Ambite-Arrese-Cendoya as a multi-purpose athletics venue seating around 32,000, replacing Real Sociedad's previous home, Atotxa. For over two decades, fans complained that the surrounding running track kept them too far from the pitch, dulling the atmosphere despite passionate Basque support.

A comprehensive renovation, led by local architect Izaskun Larzabal (who also designed much of Real Sociedad's Zubieta training complex), finally addressed that problem between 2017 and 2019: the athletics track was removed entirely, the pitch lowered by 240cm, and both side stands rebuilt as a single tier just 8 metres from the pitch at its closest point, first-ever rectangular ends replacing the old rounded corners.

A translucent inner roof, supported by self-sustaining steel trusses mounted atop the existing structure, lets 25% of daylight through to keep public areas naturally lit while sheltering fans from San Sebastián's frequent rain. The renovation was originally budgeted at €47 million, split between Real Sociedad (€33m), the Basque Government (€10m) and the Gipuzkoa Provincial Council (€4m), though more recent reporting puts the final completed cost closer to €78 million once overruns are included, a very different funding model from San Mamés's ground-up rebuild in nearby Bilbao.

Work began in May 2017 with the demolition of the south end's external staircases and curve, followed by a new grandstand that moved the front row 35 metres closer to the pitch, opened early in the 2018-19 season. From May 2018, the lower east and west sides were reconfigured to bring fans a further 15 metres nearer the action, with capacity temporarily dropping to around 25,000-26,800 mid-project before the full renovation was completed in October 2019.

The overhaul also introduced a hospitality tier that simply didn't exist before: 2,300 hospitality seats across 82 executive boxes, alongside a broader shift in who attends matches, the club has reported the share of women in the crowd rising from roughly 18% to 26% since the rebuild, alongside a noticeably younger season-ticket base.

Capacity Expansion

Racing to Clear FIFA's Bar

Reale Arena's 2019 renovation left it at roughly 39,500-40,177 seats, comfortable for La Liga but short of FIFA's expectation of a solid surplus above 40,000 for World Cup venues.

Real Sociedad president Jokin Aperribay has been working with San Sebastián's city council, which owns the stadium, on a further expansion specifically to secure the club's World Cup ambitions. Major construction is scheduled to begin in May 2026, once the 2025-26 season ends, focused on the upper ring above the east and west stands and the currently empty corners, though some pitch-adjacent modifications may start earlier depending on permits.

Even reaching 44,000 seats, Larzabal has said, would be achievable without major structural work, just modest changes to the east stand and filling in the corners where stands are currently absent. The expansion is also intended to chip away at a season-ticket waiting list that has topped 3,000 fans.

What We Don't Know Yet

A notable, unusual proposal has surfaced: the Basque Country has floated having San Mamés and Reale Arena operate as a single combined World Cup host venue to reduce costs and community impact. This remains an unconfirmed idea rather than a formal decision, and separately, some San Sebastián neighbourhood groups have formally asked FIFA to withdraw the city as a host, citing overtourism concerns. We'll update this page as either situation develops.

Matchday Atmosphere

Closer to the Pitch, Louder for It

Removing the athletics track didn't just change sightlines, it transformed the atmosphere, bringing fans as close as 8 metres from the pitch and turning a once-distant bowl into one of Spain's more intimate top-flight grounds.

The stadium's official singing section carries the name of Aitor Zabaleta, a young Real Sociedad supporter murdered by a rival hooligan outside Atlético Madrid's stadium in 1998, a tragedy that helped spur Spanish football's anti-violence reforms. Renaming a stand in his memory turned a painful chapter into a lasting, peaceful fixture of matchday culture rather than a flashpoint, and the section remains one of the loudest parts of the ground today.

The Basque derby against Athletic Bilbao, hosts of San Mamés, remains the standout fixture on the calendar, and the 2019-20 Copa del Rey win, Real Sociedad's first trophy in 34 years, arrived just months after the renovation transformed both the sightlines and the noise inside the ground.

History & Significance

Real Sociedad's Third Home

Reale Arena is Real Sociedad's third stadium, following Ondarreta (1909-1913) and Atotxa (1913-1993), and remains one of nine confirmed Spanish venues for the 2030 World Cup.

Athletic Bilbao even briefly used the ground for a 2013-14 season fixture while San Mamés was under construction, and the Basque unofficial regional team has played friendlies there in 1993, 1998, 2004 and 2012. Reale Arena sits alongside San Mamés in nearby Bilbao and Camp Nou in Barcelona on Spain's 21-venue World Cup list, which also includes Morocco's Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium in Rabat and Portugal's Estádio José Alvalade in Lisbon.

A Basque Cup Final

The stadium is the home of San Sebastián's Real Sociedad, founded in 1909. Reale Arena also hosted the 2019-20 UEFA Women's Champions League final and has staged Top 14 rugby fixtures for nearby French clubs Biarritz Olympique and Aviron Bayonnais.

Nicknamed "La Real" and known by fans as Txuri-urdin, Basque for "blue and white," the club draws heavily on its Zubieta academy, one of Spain's most productive youth systems, which has produced players including Xabi Alonso, Antoine Griezmann and Mikel Oyarzabal. Real Sociedad also holds the distinction of being the first Spanish club to field an all-Basque starting eleven in the modern era, a point of pride tied closely to the region's football identity.

World Cup 2030 Role

A Basque Pairing with San Mamés

Reale Arena's path to 2030 hosting has been more contested than most Spanish venues: its capacity fell short of FIFA's practical minimum, prompting the current expansion push.

Separately, some local neighbourhood associations have formally written to FIFA asking San Sebastián to withdraw as a host city over overtourism concerns. Neither issue has derailed the bid so far, but both are worth watching as 2030 approaches.

Alongside San Mamés in Bilbao, Reale Arena gives the Basque Country two 2030 venues within roughly an hour of each other, and the unusual proposal to potentially combine both bids into a single joint host arrangement reflects just how closely linked the two cities' World Cup ambitions have become.

Frequently Asked

Reale Arena Questions

Who designed Reale Arena?
The original 1993 stadium was designed by architects Ambite-Arrese-Cendoya. The 2017-2019 transformation into a football-specific venue was led by local architect Izaskun Larzabal.
Why did Reale Arena have an athletics track?
The stadium, originally called Anoeta, was built in 1993 as a multi-purpose venue combining athletics and football, a common approach at the time that later frustrated fans wanting a more intimate football experience.
How was the athletics track removed?
The pitch was lowered by 240cm and the lower tier of the existing stands demolished, bringing fans as close as 8 metres from the pitch, with both ends rebuilt in rectangular form for the first time.
What is distinctive about the roof?
A translucent inner roof supported by self-sustaining steel trusses, with 25% translucency, keeping concourses naturally lit during the day while sheltering fans from San Sebastián's frequent rain.
What is the current capacity of Reale Arena?
40,177 seats following the 2019 renovation, up from an original 32,076.
Why is Reale Arena expanding further?
To meet FIFA's practical expectation of a solid surplus above 40,000 seats for World Cup venues, leaving room for press, broadcasters and hospitality. The expansion, announced in December 2024, targets 42,247 seats at a cost of €10.6 million, with major construction beginning in May 2026.
Could the stadium expand even further?
The renovation architect has said reaching 42,000-44,000 seats would not require major structural work, just modest changes to the east stand and filling in the currently empty corners.
What kind of pitch does Reale Arena have?
A hybrid grass system installed during the 2019 renovation, combining around 95% natural turf with 5% reinforcing synthetic fibres for improved durability and faster recovery between matches.
When did Reale Arena open?
13 August 1993, originally named Anoeta, replacing Real Sociedad's previous home, Atotxa Stadium.
What club plays at Reale Arena?
Real Sociedad, founded in 1909, San Sebastián's leading club and one of Spain's most storied Basque teams.
Did Real Sociedad win anything after the renovation?
Yes, the club won the 2019-20 Copa del Rey, ending a 34-year trophy drought shortly after the stadium's transformation improved the matchday atmosphere and revenue.
Has Reale Arena hosted major finals before?
Yes, the 2019-20 UEFA Women's Champions League final, plus Top 14 rugby fixtures for French clubs Biarritz Olympique and Aviron Bayonnais.
How do I get to Reale Arena?
The Anoeta station on the E2 line of Euskotren Trena serves the stadium directly, roughly 15 minutes from the Parte Vieja or La Concha beach, alongside several bus routes.
What's the nearest airport to Reale Arena?
San Sebastián Airport (EAS) is small with limited connections. Most fans fly into Bilbao Airport (BIO) instead, roughly an hour away by road.
Where should I stay for a match at Reale Arena?
The Parte Vieja (Old Town) or near La Concha beach offer the best combination of culture, food and easy access, roughly 15 minutes by bus or a scenic 30-minute walk. See our full San Sebastián guide for more.
Is San Sebastián's World Cup hosting confirmed?
The city remains on Spain's official venue list, but some local neighbourhood groups have formally asked FIFA to withdraw San Sebastián as a host over overtourism concerns. Nothing has changed the official status as of this writing.
