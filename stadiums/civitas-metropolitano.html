Architecture & Design

From Athletics Bid to Atlético's Home

The stadium's design traces back to 1988, when Cruz y Ortiz Arquitectos, led by Antonio Ortiz, first drew plans for what became the Estadio Olímpico de Madrid, built for Madrid's unsuccessful bid for the 1997 World Athletics Championships and opened in 1994. The venue closed in 2004 after Madrid's failed 2016 Olympic bid, before Atlético Madrid acquired it in 2013 and rebuilt it as their new home, reopening on 16 September 2017 as Atlético beat Málaga 1-0 in front of King Felipe VI, with Antoine Griezmann scoring the stadium's first goal.

Converting a track-and-field stadium into a football-specific venue meant digging the pitch further into the ground, sinking it roughly 15 metres below street level. The result places the pitch just 6-9 metres from the first row at its closest points, dramatically better sightlines than Atlético's previous home, the Vicente Calderón, even if the distance grows significantly by the upper tiers.

The athletics-to-football conversion puts Cívitas Metropolitano in good company among 2030 host venues: Morocco's Ibn Batouta Stadium and Adrar Stadium both removed their own athletics tracks during recent renovations to bring fans closer to the pitch, a shared design instinct across otherwise very different projects. Unlike Santiago Bernabéu, which Real Madrid renovated while continuing to play there, Atlético took the opposite route, staying at the ageing Vicente Calderón throughout construction before making a clean break to the finished venue in 2017.

Beyond the bowl itself, the conversion added more than 11,000 square metres of VIP and event space, including a 400-guest auditorium and some of Madrid's most modern convention facilities, turning what began as a purely athletic venue into a genuine year-round commercial complex.

The old Vicente Calderón didn't survive the move. Once Atlético relocated, the club sold the riverside site, the stadium was demolished, and its footprint is now home to an apartment development along the Manzanares, closing the loop on a financing plan originally tied to that very plot two decades earlier.

Sinking the pitch 15 metres also created a genuine engineering challenge around drainage and groundwater, given the site's proximity to the M-40 ring road and existing underground infrastructure. Solving it required specialist foundation work most fans never think about, buried beneath the same turf they watch from the stands every matchday.

The stadium's original financing plan, swapping land under the old Vicente Calderón and a nearby brewery site for private development funding, collapsed with the 2008 Spanish financial crisis, forcing Atlético to put forward substantial funds directly, including an initial €60.3 million just for the land itself.

Engineering & Fan Experience

The Crown

The stadium's defining architectural feature is what its designers call "the crown," a gently undulating roof built from 96 PTFE segments that creates a striking origami-like impression from inside while keeping the structure's height to just 32-42 metres.

The stadium has hosted events well beyond football, including rugby internationals, the 2018 Copa del Rey final (Barcelona 5-0 Sevilla), a women's football world attendance record (60,739 for Atlético Madrid Femenino vs Barcelona in 2019), and La Velada del Año, an annual celebrity boxing event that drew over 70,000 fans in 2023, Spain's largest boxing crowd since 1930. A concert Hall of Fame, Sala Manzanares, opened for the 2025-26 season, honouring artists from Bruce Springsteen to the Rolling Stones who have played the venue and its predecessor, the Vicente Calderón.

Capacity figures also vary slightly by source, from 70,460 after the September 2023 expansion to 70,692 following further Skybox and accessibility additions. Matchday Atmosphere

Coraje y Corazón

Atlético Madrid's identity, red and white in colour and famously scrappy in spirit, is stamped across the stadium's southwest corner in a 338-square-metre flag, the biggest in Spain, next to text reading "Coraje y Corazón" (Courage and Heart).

Find Madrid Hotels See Spain Guide

Matchdays here revolve around what's often called "Cholismo," the gritty, all-in football identity built by long-serving coach Diego Simeóne, a mentality Atlético fans have embraced as a point of pride against the bigger budgets across town at Real Madrid. The stadium's south and north ends each have dedicated fan zones, the south hosting a concert stage and concession stands, the north set aside specifically for families.

The Madrid Derby against Real Madrid remains the fixture that defines the calendar, a genuine rivalry rather than a mismatch in recent years, with both sides having claimed major trophies against the other. Beyond the derby, the stadium's acoustics, engineered specifically to project crowd noise, give even routine league fixtures a notably loud, compact atmosphere compared to many larger European grounds.

Street food outside the ground follows familiar matchday patterns, bocadillos and churros from vendors along the approach roads, alongside the stadium's own expanded concession offerings inside.

History & Significance

A Rare Repeat Champions League Host

The stadium has carried several names since its 1994 opening as Estadio Olímpico de Madrid, and now hosts two Champions League finals within a single decade, a genuinely rare distinction in European football.

Madrid Guide All World Cup Stadiums

Its names trace the venue's evolving ownership: Estadio de la Comunidad de Madrid, the popular nickname "La Peineta" (the comb, for its distinctive silhouette), Wanda Metropolitano under a Chinese sponsorship deal from 2016, Cívitas Metropolitano, and now Riyadh Air Metropolitano since a October 2024 naming-rights deal, though UEFA regulations require the neutral "Estadio Metropolitano" for European nights.

Cívitas Metropolitano is a candidate to host 2030 World Cup matches, one of the stadiums Spain proposed to FIFA, alongside Santiago Bernabéu and Camp Nou.

World Cup 2030 Role

Madrid's Second Stage

As Spain's third-largest stadium behind Camp Nou and Santiago Bernabéu, Cívitas Metropolitano is expected to host quarter-final or semi-final level matches at the 2030 World Cup rather than the final itself, though nothing has been formally confirmed by FIFA.

Its recent hosting of the 2019 Champions League final, plus its confirmed return for the 2027 final, gives organisers a venue with genuine, proven experience running the biggest club matches in European football. Madrid uniquely offers two 2030 World Cup stadium candidates within the same city, alongside Santiago Bernabéu, giving fans based in the Spanish capital a real chance of attending multiple matches without changing cities.

Its capacity sits in a similar bracket to Morocco's Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium and Portugal's Estádio José Alvalade, both solid mid-to-large tournament venues rather than final contenders, a useful benchmark for fans planning a trip across all three host nations.

Frequently Asked

Cívitas Metropolitano Questions

Who designed Cívitas Metropolitano?
Cruz y Ortiz Arquitectos, led by Antonio Ortiz, first drew plans in 1988 for what became the Estadio Olímpico de Madrid. The stadium's distinctive wave-shaped roof was engineered by Schlaich Bergermann Partner.
Why was the stadium originally built as an athletics venue?
It was constructed as part of Madrid's unsuccessful bid to host the 1997 World Athletics Championships, opening in 1994 as the Estadio Olímpico de Madrid before later becoming Atlético Madrid's home.
How was the athletics stadium converted for football?
The pitch was dug roughly 15 metres into the ground, bringing the field as close as 6-9 metres from the first row at its nearest points, far closer than Atlético's previous home, the Vicente Calderón.
What is "the crown"?
The nickname for the stadium's roof, a gently undulating cover made from 96 PTFE segments that create an origami-like appearance from inside, kept deliberately low (32-42 metres) to avoid dominating the surrounding landscape.
What is the capacity of Cívitas Metropolitano?
Roughly 70,700 seats as of the most recent expansions, up from 70,460 after the September 2023 expansion and 68,456 at the 2017 reopening. Figures vary slightly by source depending on the most recent Skybox and accessibility additions counted.
Are further renovations planned?
Yes, architect Antonio Ortiz has confirmed a third renovation phase covering a new facade and improved acoustics, though no timeline or budget has been published as of this writing. A separate AI-managed network upgrade with HPE is already underway through 2027.
Was this really the first all-LED stadium in the world?
Yes, at the time of its 2017 reopening, Cívitas Metropolitano was described as the first stadium in the world equipped entirely with LED lighting and a 360-degree LED ribbon board, since upgraded to a 404-metre LED ribbon built with Telefónica and LG.
When did Atlético Madrid move into this stadium?
16 September 2017, beating Málaga 1-0 in front of King Felipe VI, with Antoine Griezmann scoring the stadium's first goal.
Has the stadium hosted a Champions League final?
Yes, the 2019 final, in which Liverpool beat Tottenham Hotspur 2-0. The stadium is also confirmed to host the 2027 final, a rare repeat honour, plus the 2018 Copa del Rey final between Barcelona and Sevilla.
What was the stadium called before?
Estadio Olímpico de Madrid (1994), Estadio de la Comunidad de Madrid, and the popular nickname "La Peineta." It later became Wanda Metropolitano (2016-2022) under Chinese sponsorship, then Cívitas Metropolitano, and since October 2024, Riyadh Air Metropolitano.
What other major events has the stadium hosted?
A women's football world attendance record (60,739 for a 2019 Atlético Madrid Femenino vs Barcelona match), rugby internationals, and La Velada del Año, a celebrity boxing event that drew over 70,000 fans in 2023.
What is "Cholismo"?
The gritty, all-in football identity associated with long-serving coach Diego Simeone, embraced by Atlético fans as a point of pride and a core part of the club's matchday culture.
How do I get to Cívitas Metropolitano?
The stadium is served directly by Madrid Metro Lines 7 and 9, roughly 25-30 minutes from central Madrid.
What's the nearest airport to Cívitas Metropolitano?
Adolfo Suárez Madrid-Barajas Airport (MAD), roughly 20-25 minutes away given the stadium's eastern Madrid location.
Where should I stay for a match at Cívitas Metropolitano?
Central Madrid works well thanks to direct Metro access via Lines 7 and 9. See our full Madrid guide for specific neighbourhood recommendations.
Can I tour the stadium outside of matchdays?
Yes, Atlético Madrid offers stadium tours from around €25, covering the changing rooms, gym facilities, press rooms, boxes, players' tunnel and dugout, plus the club museum and trophy cabinet.
